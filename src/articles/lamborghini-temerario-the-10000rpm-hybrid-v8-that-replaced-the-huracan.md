For two decades, the soundtrack of an "entry-level" Lamborghini was the howl of a naturally aspirated V10 — first in the Gallardo, then the Huracán. So when Sant'Agata announced its successor, the big question was never about power. It was about character. Could a turbocharged, electrified Lamborghini still feel like a Lamborghini? The answer is the **Temerario**, and it is far more interesting than a simple spec sheet suggests.

### The end of the V10 era

Let's address the elephant in the room: the Temerario marks the end of Lamborghini's beloved naturally aspirated V10. For purists, that stings. The V10 was one of the great engines of the modern era, defining the Gallardo and Huracán across two decades. But emissions regulations and the relentless march toward electrification meant its days were always numbered. Rather than mourn, Lamborghini has tried to channel that emotion into something new — and the engineering it has produced is genuinely remarkable.

### A clean-sheet V8 that spins to 10,000rpm

At the heart of the Temerario sits an all-new 4.0-litre twin-turbocharged V8, designed and built from scratch at Sant'Agata Bolognese. You might assume it shares parts with the V8 in the Urus SUV, but it does not — this is a completely fresh design. The most jaw-dropping figure is its redline: the Temerario revs all the way to 10,000 rpm, making it the only production super sports car capable of doing so. In an era of low-revving turbo engines, that is an extraordinary technical achievement, and it preserves a slice of the high-rpm drama that made the V10 so special. The V8 alone produces 800 CV between 9,000 and 9,750 rpm.

### Three electric motors, one serious punch

The V8 does not work alone. Three electric motors join it — two on the front axle, each driving an individual front wheel, and one integrated at the rear between the engine and gearbox. Drawing from a compact lithium-ion battery, they lift combined output to around 907 hp (920 CV in Lamborghini's measure). The benefits go beyond raw numbers: the front motors enable torque vectoring for sharper cornering, the rear motor fills in low-down torque while the turbos spool, and the whole system can even run as a front-wheel-drive EV for short, silent city trips. The result is a car that sprints from 0 to 100 km/h in 2.7 seconds and tops out beyond 340 km/h.

### Built lighter, stiffer, smarter

Underpinning all of this is a redesigned aluminium spaceframe that increases torsional stiffness by more than 20 percent over the Huracán, using a new high-strength alloy that better accommodates the hybrid hardware. Clever manufacturing — high-pressure castings, hydroformed sections and hollow castings — has cut the component count by more than half and reduced weld length by over 80 percent. The bodywork and underbody have been reworked from scratch for aerodynamics, with the optional Alleggerita package boosting downforce dramatically for owners who want maximum track focus.

### Does it still feel like a Lambo?

Early impressions suggest the Temerario's electric motors amplify rather than tame its character. It is loud, aggressive and theatrical, with the hybrid system adding immediacy rather than sterility. The one criticism that keeps surfacing is sound — some testers feel the V8 simply does not shout as loudly as the old V10 did, even if what is there is excellent. That is perhaps the one piece of the V10's magic that no amount of clever engineering can fully replace. Everywhere else, though, the Temerario is a more capable, more sophisticated and more usable supercar than the car it replaces. It is the sound of Lamborghini stepping into a new era — and refusing to do so quietly.
